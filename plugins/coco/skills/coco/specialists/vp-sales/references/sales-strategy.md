# Sales strategy: choose the buyer, offer and next move

Use this guide when choosing a sales direction, refining targeting, diagnosing
growth, or deciding where to spend the next week. Start from the existing company
brief and results. Produce a decision and useful work, not another intake form.

## Find the constraint

Trace the path from reachable buyers to conversations, qualified meetings,
opportunities, wins and successful delivery. Find the earliest material weakness
supported by data. Few relevant accounts suggests targeting; conversations without
qualified meetings suggests the offer or qualification; meetings without progress
suggest discovery, proof or the buying process. Check delivery capacity before
increasing demand. Missing evidence is a research task, not a diagnosis.

Use the customer's definitions and sales cycle. A signup goal may need activation
work; a revenue goal may need existing deals advanced before more leads sourced.
Choose one primary constraint for the current work cycle.

## Define fit through the problem

Compare good customers with losses, churn and difficult delivery. Look for the
problem, buyer role, business model, operating conditions and reason for buying.
Industry and headcount are useful filters, but rarely explain the purchase alone.
Small or biased samples produce hypotheses; do not overfit to one large win.

Write a compact working definition:

> We help **[role]** at **[business type]** achieve **[outcome]** when
> **[specific situation]** makes **[problem]** costly. They fit when
> **[must-haves]** are evidenced. We exclude **[disqualifiers]**.

Separate the user, internal advocate, budget owner and approval participants.
An operator who shares useful material can be valuable without owning budget.
For an unfamiliar vertical, confirm who actually performs and pays for the work;
it may be an intermediary rather than the obvious end customer.

Build lookalikes from desirable customers' problems, offers, buyers and delivery
conditions. Check each candidate's business model on its own website. Superficial
similarity does not establish the same need. With no customers yet, start from
product capability and buyer research, and explicitly label the ICP provisional.

## Keep four judgments separate

| Question | Evidence | Effect on the plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can we help? | Must-have fit and problem relevance | Include, exclude or investigate |
| Why now? | Dated request, change or relevant behavior | Priority and timing |
| Can we reach the right person? | Current role and usable channel | Channel choice |
| Is the effort worthwhile? | Plausible value, delivery cost and team capacity | Research depth and attention |

Missing email is a reachability gap, not poor company fit. A funding announcement
is context, not a confirmed budget. Do not add arbitrary points to hide uncertainty.
If the company has a scoring rubric, show its reasons, preserve unknowns, and
check it against outcomes it was not built from. Use information available at the
decision date; later facts must not make an old prediction appear better.

Reserve individual research for valuable, strong-fit accounts the team can
actually service. Use repeatable segment work for other confirmed fits. Uncertain
accounts need investigation or a bounded test; disqualified accounts do not enter
a cheap volume campaign.

## Make the offer concrete

Connect a buyer problem to a useful result and a credible mechanism. Prefer
something the buyer can inspect: a relevant demonstration, comparison, short
diagnosis, worked example or actual customer result. Choose the smallest useful
next step for their readiness. An explicit demo request deserves a demo, not a
forced resource exchange.

Use this brief before drafting:

```text
Buyer and situation:
Problem we can address:
What we do differently, and why that helps:
Evidence we may use:
Useful thing offered now:
One next action:
What we cannot promise:
```

If proof is absent, demonstrate the work or propose an honest test. Never borrow
someone else's results as the customer's track record. Do the promised sample
before saying it exists. Match free work to account value and delivery capacity.

## Choose a manageable route

Pick the channel where the buyer is reachable and the offer can be demonstrated.
Targeted outreach can test a narrow problem; partners, referrals and events can
provide relevant access. Coordinate with the CMO for LinkedIn publishing and
audience development or search content supporting the sales goal. Choose from evidence, not a fixed
vendor stack. Organic work still costs time.

Default to one main experiment and only the supporting activity it needs. If the
user selects multiple channels, prepare a bounded experiment for each; prioritize
execution without dropping their chosen scope. Keep a working channel running
while testing an addition. Multiple channels should share
account identity, exclusions, ownership and history. Do not require a complex
system before the first useful conversation.

Use [outbound and pipeline](outbound-pipeline.md) for prospect research, individual
sales messages and replies. Hand LinkedIn content, public engagement and paid
distribution to the CMO with the audience, offer, evidence and authorized scope. Add automation to a repeated bottleneck after the workflow works.

## Run an experiment that answers a question

Define one segment, one offer hypothesis, a bounded cohort, an owner, budget or
effort ceiling, and a review date suited to the sales cycle. State what evidence
would justify continuing, changing or stopping. A small pilot checks execution
and surfaces objections; it does not prove a reliable conversion rate.

Keep targeting and message changes distinguishable. Avoid splitting a tiny list
across many variants. Read accepted and rejected account examples, actual replies
and downstream qualification before scaling. Update the brief with the finding,
date and scope; repeated source anecdotes are not independent validation.

## Turn conversations into sound deals

Prepare discovery from known facts. Ask only what remains unresolved:

> “How does this work today? Where does it cause trouble? What would an
> improvement need to change? Who needs to evaluate it, and what happens next?”

Confirm the buyer's own measures, constraints and decision process. A proposal
should connect their problem to scope, deliverables, success criteria, dependencies,
commercial terms and a mutual next step. Do not promise unapproved prices or dates.

Draft the follow-up in this shape:

> “My understanding is that **[problem]** is affecting **[outcome]**. We agreed
> to **[next action]**, owned by **[person]**, by **[date]**. The remaining
> question is **[decision]**. Please correct anything I misunderstood.”

Use placeholders where agreement is missing. On a win, hand delivery the actual
scope, commitments, owner, access needs and first milestone. Signature, payment,
provisioning and successful onboarding are distinct states.

## Review outcomes and decide

Compare dated cohorts and show numerator/denominator: positive replies per unique
delivered prospect, qualified held meetings per contacted account, wins per mature
qualified opportunity. Keep booked and held meetings separate. Report unavailable
for missing denominators; do not infer failure from an immature sales cycle.

Track acquisition effort and cost per qualified outcome alongside revenue and
delivery quality. Distinguish first-touch sourcing from assisted influence; count
a deal's revenue once. Exposure correlations do not prove a channel caused a win.
Keep currencies separate and forecasts visibly conditional.

Return a short brief: the constraint, evidence, decision, completed deliverable
and next action with an owner. Continue authorized work; do not turn each step
into another approval request.
